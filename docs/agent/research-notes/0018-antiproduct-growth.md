# 0018 The growth of the geometric antiproduct at fixed use: inherent or construction?

- Date: 2026-11-01
- Author: Claude (agent, Opus 5.5), in the role of math-expert (researcher), at the owner's request after the WU7b outcome (plan 0013, decision 0116).
- Branch: `research/antiproduct-growth`, from `develop` at `c4b2e70`. The antiproduct plans it studies are those of `feature/core-plan-maps` at `50c7c70`, read only.
- Question: WU7b builds the geometric antiproduct by complement conjugation, a ⟇ b = left(right(a) ⟑ right(b)) (CONVENTIONS 4.2). A fixed use (four typed rigid(3) pairs embedded in R(n−1,0,1)) costs +268% (GCC) and +463% (Clang) more compile-time instructions at n = 16 than at n = 4, where the geometric product stays nearly flat. Is the growth inherent in the algebra, which P2 and decision 0005 admit, or an artifact of the construction, a defect against C6? And can the antiproduct be generated by the one mechanism with different per-basis data (C4), or does it need something new?

## Answer

The growth is an artifact of the construction. Mathematically, the antiproduct of blades on a set of generators C that is closed under the form's coupling (i ~ j when g_ij ≠ 0) is the antiproduct of the algebra on C alone, times one scalar, the square γ of the blade of the other generators, and per-blade signs (Theorem 2). So it can be generated by the same `blade_product` and the same conjugation, run with complements inside C instead of inside all n generators, plus that scalar and those signs: composition of the one mechanism with exact per-basis data (masks, signs, one coefficient), which is what C4 asks. For the measured use, C is the four generators of rigid(3) at every n, so the work per pair is that of rigid(3) at every n: the generator's loop count for the four pairs is 1,959 at every n from 4 to 16 instead of rising from 2,020 to 23,692 (section 2), and the term lists need capacity 8 instead of 2^(n−1) = 32,768.

What is inherent, and stays (section 4):

1. The antiproduct's value depends on generators that neither input names: e_1 ⟇ e_1 is 0 in R(n−1,0,1) and −𝟙 in R(n,0,0). So any correct method reads something about every generator outside C at least once. That read is the scalar γ, one pass over the form, once per algebra or per plan, never per term. It is O(n) to O(n²) per algebra, inside the O(n²) that decision 0005 admits for the signature data.
2. In a form whose coupling graph connects the use to many generators, the result itself grows with that component's size: in a dense form e_1 ⟇ e_2 has up to 1 + C(n,2) terms while e_1 ⟑ e_2 has 2. There C is large and nothing can avoid the cost. This growth follows from the sparsity pattern of the signature, which is the exception P2 and decision 0005 name.

The reading "multiply the original blades with the antimetric in place of the metric" is not the antiproduct (section 1.4), so the direct route that works is not that one: it is conjugation restricted to the coupling closure. A prototype of it on the WU7b generator, outside `include/`, equals WU7b's plan on every case checked. Measured on decision 0116's use, the antiproduct's growth from n = 4 to 16 falls from +265% to +9% on GCC and from +464% to +11% on Clang; with the coupling data kept per algebra, what the plans cost grows by +0.7% and +4.3%, and the rest is one O(n²) read of the form per algebra (sections 5 and 6).

## 1. What the antiproduct of two basis blades is

Notation. Generators e_1 … e_n, Gram matrix g, any symmetric integer form. A blade e_X is the wedge of its generators in increasing order (CONVENTIONS 1.1). τ(X, Y) is the sign of reordering e_X e_Y, (−1) to the number of pairs x ∈ X, y ∈ Y with x > y (`reorder_sign`), so e_X ∧ e_Y = τ(X, Y) e_(X ∪ Y) for disjoint X and Y. X̄ is the complement of X in all n generators. right and left are the complements of CONVENTIONS 3.1, with right(e_X) = r(X) e_X̄, r(X) = τ(X, X̄), and left(e_X) = l(X) e_X̄, l(X) = τ(X̄, X).

### 1.1 The antiproduct is the geometric product of the same form on antivectors

**Theorem 1.** right carries the geometric product to the antiproduct, right(a ⟑ b) = right(a) ⟇ right(b), so the antivectors ē_i = right(e_i) satisfy ē_i ⟇ ē_j + ē_j ⟇ ē_i = 2 g_ij 𝟙, and (Λ, ⟇) is the Clifford algebra of the same form g with unit 𝟙, generated by the antivectors.

*Proof.* The De Morgan law is proved in decision 0115 (its "Proofs at the level of plans"; the decision is on `feature/core-plan-maps`, not yet on `develop`): right(a) ⟇ right(b) = left(right(right(a)) ⟑ right(right(b))) and the signs (−1)^(gr·ag) cancel because the product preserves grade parity. Applying right to e_i e_j + e_j e_i = 2 g_ij gives the relation, since right(1) = 𝟙. ∎

This is Lengyel's statement, read above: the metric does not change, the generators do. It is also exactly WU7b's construction: the "antimetric formulation" with the Gram data on complements is the conjugation, with the same per-basis data (complement masks and complement signs). The cost follows: in the antialgebra's own generators the vector e_i is an antiwedge of n − 1 antivectors, so a generator that recurses over the generators of a factor recurses over n − |A| of them. So the antimetric formulation the owner named first is not a different route; it is the one WU7b took.

### 1.2 Every form: reduction to the coupling closure

Call generators i ≠ j coupled when g_ij ≠ 0, and call the connected components of this graph the components of the form. In R(p,q,r) every generator is its own component; in conformal(d) the origin and infinity form one component of two; a Witt form has one component per pair; a dense form has one component.

**Theorem 2 (orthogonal reduction).** Let C be a union of components and Q the other generators, so g_cq = 0 for c ∈ C, q ∈ Q. Let γ = e_Q e_Q. For A, B ⊆ C let e_A ⟇_C e_B = Σ_E a_E e_E be the antiproduct of the algebra on C alone (complements inside C, pseudoscalar e_C). Then

    e_A ⟇ e_B = γ · (−1)^(|Q|(|C| + |A|)) · τ(C, Q) · Σ_E a_E τ(E, Q) e_(E ∪ Q),

and γ = (−1)^(|Q|(|Q|−1)/2) det(g_QQ), the product of the components' determinants times that sign.

The proof rests on three facts.

- *Fact 1: orthogonal blades multiply as wedges.* For X ⊆ C and Y ⊆ Q, e_X e_Y = e_X ∧ e_Y. Expanding e_X by `blade_product`'s recursion, every contraction against a generator of Y carries a factor g_cq = 0.
- *Fact 2: orthogonal parts graded-commute.* A generator of C and one of Q anticommute, since e_c e_q + e_q e_c = 2 g_cq = 0. A blade of C is a sum of products of generators of C, all with its grade's parity, and likewise for Q, so y x = (−1)^(pr) x y for parities p and r.
- *Fact 3: the square of a blade.* e_K e_K = (−1)^(|K|(|K|−1)/2) det(g_KK): diagonalize the form on the span of K and argue as for the pseudoscalar (knowledge-base note on the pseudoscalar square, ga-E5). If K splits into orthogonal parts, g_KK is block diagonal and its determinant the product of the blocks'.

*Proof.* Write R_A = r_C(A) e_(C∖A), the right complement of e_A inside C, so e_A ∧ R_A = e_C.

1. e_A ∧ (R_A ∧ e_Q) = e_C ∧ e_Q = τ(C, Q) 𝟙, so right(e_A) = τ(C, Q) R_A ∧ e_Q = τ(C, Q) R_A e_Q by Fact 1. Likewise for B.
2. right(e_A) right(e_B) = R_A e_Q R_B e_Q (the two τ(C, Q) multiply to 1). Moving e_Q past R_B, of grade |C| − |B|, gives (−1)^(|Q|(|C|−|B|)) by Fact 2, so the product is (−1)^(|Q|(|C|−|B|)) γ R_A R_B.
3. R_A R_B = Σ_D c_D e_D with every D ⊆ C, and |D| ≡ |A| + |B| (mod 2), since the product preserves grade parity.
4. For D ⊆ C and E = C ∖ D: (e_E ∧ e_Q) ∧ e_D = (−1)^(|Q||D|) (e_E ∧ e_D) ∧ e_Q = (−1)^(|Q||D|) l_C(D) τ(C, Q) 𝟙. So left(e_D) = (−1)^(|Q||D|) l_C(D) τ(C, Q) τ(E, Q) e_(E ∪ Q).
5. Inside C alone the same steps with Q empty give a_E = r_C(A) r_C(B) c_D l_C(D). In all n generators the coefficient on e_(E ∪ Q) is the product of the factors of steps 1 to 4, γ a_E (−1)^(|Q|(|C| − |B| + |D|)) τ(C, Q) τ(E, Q), and |D| ≡ |A| + |B| turns the exponent into |Q|(|C| + |A|). ∎

Two properties the plan generator relies on carry over. The outputs of one pair, read from the last term of the sub-product, are in canonical order: adding the same disjoint Q to every blade shifts every grade by |Q| and keeps every lowest differing generator, and inside C the complement reverses canonical order (decision 0115). And any union of components holding A and B may serve as C, so one C, the closure of all blades of both input sets, serves a whole plan.

WU7b's construction is the case C = all generators, Q = ∅, γ = 1, every extra sign +1.

The antiwedge follows the same way with the zero form (the wedge reads no metric, so every generator is its own component and its "square" is 0): e_A ∨ e_B = 0 unless A ∪ B is every generator. A low-grade antiwedge plan in a large algebra is empty, as WU7b's sparse limit units found.

### 1.3 Diagonal forms: a sign and a mask from the antimetric diagonal

**Theorem 3 (closed diagonal form).** In a diagonal form,

    e_A ⟇ e_B = τ(B̄, Ā) · ∏_(j ∉ A ∪ B) g_jj · e_(complement of A xor B),

and the factor ∏_(j ∉ A ∪ B) g_jj is the diagonal entry of the antimetric 𝔾 on the blade of A ∪ B.

*Proof, step by step.*

1. In a diagonal form two blades multiply to one term: e_X e_Y = τ(X, Y) ∏_(j ∈ X ∩ Y) g_jj e_(X xor Y). (Each common generator meets its copy and squares; reordering gives τ; this is the rule `reorder_sign` documents.)
2. By the definition, e_A ⟇ e_B = r(A) r(B) left(e_Ā e_B̄). With X = Ā and Y = B̄: Ā ∩ B̄ is the set of generators in neither A nor B, and Ā xor B̄ = A xor B. So e_Ā e_B̄ = τ(Ā, B̄) ∏_(j ∉ A ∪ B) g_jj e_(A xor B), and its left complement carries l(A xor B) and lands on the complement of A xor B.
3. The sign is r(A) r(B) τ(Ā, B̄) l(A xor B). Write β(X, Y) for the parity of τ(X, Y), a number 0 or 1. Think of a mask as a vector of bits; then β is bilinear over the two-element field, in each argument separately, because the number of out-of-order pairs of a union of disjoint sets is the sum of the counts. Complementing a mask adds the all-ones vector, call it 1. So r(A) = β(A, A + 1), l(D) = β(D + 1, D), and τ(Ā, B̄) = β(A + 1, B + 1).
4. Expand with D = A + B. The sum β(A, A+1) + β(B, B+1) + β(A+1, B+1) + β(A+B+1, A+B) is, term by term,
   β(A,A) + β(A,1) + β(B,B) + β(B,1) + β(A,B) + β(A,1) + β(1,B) + β(1,1) + β(A,A) + β(A,B) + β(B,A) + β(B,B) + β(1,A) + β(1,B).
   Every term that appears twice cancels (1 + 1 = 0): β(A,A), β(B,B), β(A,B), β(A,1), β(1,B). What remains is β(B,A) + β(B,1) + β(1,A) + β(1,1) = β(B + 1, A + 1), the parity of τ(B̄, Ā).
5. The antimetric: 𝔾 e_S = left(G right(e_S)) = r(S) l(S̄) G(S̄) e_S, and r(S) l(S̄) = 1 because left undoes right. In a diagonal form G(S̄) = ∏_(j ∈ S̄) g_jj. With S = A ∪ B this is the factor of step 2. ∎

Compare the product's rule: e_A ⟑ e_B = τ(A, B) · G(A ∩ B) · e_(A xor B), with G(A ∩ B) = ∏_(j ∈ A ∩ B) g_jj. The antiproduct reads the antimetric diagonal 𝔾 at A ∪ B, the product reads the metric diagonal G at A ∩ B. With word operations both are a fixed number of steps: τ is the parity of popcount(p & y) for a prefix-parity word p of x, and for squares in {−1, 0, +1} the factor is 0 exactly when (null mask) & ~(A | B) is nonzero, else (−1)^popcount((negative mask) & ~(A | B)). This is the "sign and a mask computed from the antimetric diagonal" that the owner anticipated. It is a second pair rule, valid for diagonal forms only.

### 1.4 The other reading of "with the antimetric" is wrong

Reading "the same rule with the metric replaced by the antimetric" as: multiply the original masks with the antimetric on vectors 𝔤_ij = right(e_i) • right(e_j) (the signed cofactors of g) in place of g, and complement the output blade. This is not the antiproduct.

Counterexample, rigid(3): 𝔤 = diag(0, 0, 0, 1), so e_14 ⟑_𝔤 e_14 = −𝔤_11 𝔤_44 = 0, while e_14 ⟇ e_14 = −𝟙 (the complements are ±e_23, and e_23 e_23 = −1). A zero cannot be repaired by any sign, so no per-basis data makes this reading correct in rigid(3). Over every form of section 3 the supports differ in every degenerate or non-diagonal form; in the forms R(p, q, 0) they agree, and per-blade signs (one per first factor, one per second, one per output blade, solved as a GF(2) system) repair the signs exactly when det g = +1 (an even number of negative squares): 15 forms yes, 12 no. The reason: for unit squares 𝔤_ii = ∏_(k≠i) g_kk = det(g) g_ii, so 𝔤 = g only when det g = 1, and Cl(−g) is in general a different algebra.

### 1.5 Conditions: when the antiproduct is the one mechanism with other data

- Every form (diagonal, degenerate, non-diagonal, conformal, Witt): yes, by Theorem 2. The mechanism is `blade_product` unchanged, on complements inside C; the per-basis data are, for an input blade A, the mask C ∖ A with sign r_C(A) (−1)^(|Q||A|); for an output term on D, the mask (C ∖ D) ∪ Q with sign l_C(D) τ(C ∖ D, Q); and for the plan, one coefficient γ (−1)^(|Q||C|) τ(C, Q). γ itself is a product of `blade_product` squares of the components outside C. Nothing new is generated; the only new data source is the coupling graph, which is read from the metric.
- Diagonal forms: also yes by Theorem 3, as a closed-form pair rule. That is a second rule beside the recursion, admitted only as a replacement under C3, and it covers diagonal forms only, so Theorem 2 is the better route.
- The original masks with the antimetric on vectors: no, except, among the forms checked, in R(p, q, 0) with q even, determinant +1 (section 1.4); nondegenerate diagonal forms with other squares were not checked.

## 2. The cost of each route

The generator's work for one pair of blades is the work of `blade_product(g, X, Y)` with X and Y the factors' masks after conjugation, plus the complement signs. `blade_product` recurses on the generators of X, one level per generator; at each level `add_generator_product` visits every term of the partial product and every generator of its blade (the contraction loop), and the outer function loops over the rest of X. In a diagonal form each partial product has one term, so a level costs about |partial blade| + |rest of X| loop steps.

- Conjugation (WU7b): X = Ā, Y = B̄. For low-grade A and B, |X| ≈ n and every partial blade has ≈ n generators, so a pair costs Θ(n²) loop steps and n + 1 − |A| calls. Every term list has the capacity of `product_capacity(Ā, B̄)` = 2^(n−1), 32,768 terms at n = 16, although each holds one term in a diagonal form.
- Reduced (Theorem 2): X = C ∖ A, Y = C ∖ B. The cost is that of the algebra on C, independent of n; the capacity is at most 2^(|C|−1). Per plan, C and γ cost one pass over the rows of the generators of C and of the components of Q: O(n |C| + n |Q|) = O(n²) reads per plan as prototyped, or O(n²) once per algebra and O(number of components) per plan if the components are kept per algebra.

Both routes give the same 68 terms at every n.

## 3. The computational check

`docs/agent/records/spikes/antiproduct-direct/check_routes.py` computes the antiproduct of every pair of basis blades of every form by the definition, with the repository's oracle: complements from `golden.complements` (from their definitions), products from `clifford_reference.Algebra` (which diagonalizes the form over fractions and multiplies in the orthogonal basis, a route the library does not take). It compares, term for term, with:

- *full*: conjugation over all n generators with the script's own transcription of `blade_product`'s recursion and its own sign functions;
- *reduced*: Theorem 2 with C the coupling closure of A ∪ B;
- *reduced+*: Theorem 2 with C enlarged by one more component;
- *closed*: Theorem 3, for diagonal forms;
- *order*: that each pair's reduced run, read from the last term, is in canonical order;
- *naive*: the reading of section 1.4, reporting support differences and the GF(2) sign test.

Forms (plan 0001 section 5, widened): every R(p,q,r) with n ≤ 6 (83 forms, which include all of section 5's diagonal list up to n = 6 and its seeded random R(p,q,r)); diag(0,1,1) and diag(0,1,1,1) with the null generator first; diag(2,−3,0,5) and diag(3,1,−2,1,0), entries other than ±1; conformal(1) to conformal(4); Witt forms with one and two modes, and two modes beside e+ and e0; the degenerate [[0,1,0],[1,0,0],[0,0,0]]; a form whose radical is not along a generator, [[1,1,0],[1,0,1],[0,1,−1]]; conformal(2) beside a null and a negative generator; e+ beside conformal(1) and two nulls; the dense forms with 2 on the diagonal and 1 elsewhere, n = 2 to 6; and the ten seeded random forms of `clifford_reference.py`. 113 forms, 168,764 pairs, 150,972 oracle terms.

Result (`out/check.txt`, 35 s through `tools/timed`): no difference on any pair for full, reduced, reduced+, closed, or order. Signs, coefficients, and which terms exist all agree. The naive reading differs in support on every degenerate or non-diagonal form and is sign-repairable on exactly the forms R(p, q, 0) with q even (determinant +1).

Also: the sign identity of Theorem 3, step 4, for every pair of masks with n = 1 to 8 (`out/sign.txt`); and the inherent parts of section 4 (`out/growth.txt`).

Independence. The oracle shares no code with the routes and multiplies by another method; the complements come from their definitions through the oracle's product. The routes share the script's transcription of the recursion, so full = oracle validates that transcription, and reduced = oracle then validates Theorem 2's signs, scalar, and closure, which the transcription does not contain. What the script does not show: that the C++ prototype matches; section 5 does that against WU7b's own generator.

## 4. Inherent or artifact: the lower bound

**Theorem 4.** Fix blade sets whose generators U have coupling closure C.

(a) Any correct method reads information about the form outside C. *Reason:* two forms that agree on C and differ outside it can give different antiproducts: R(n−1,0,1) and R(n,0,0) agree on e_1, yet e_1 ⟇ e_1 is 0 in one and −𝟙 in the other. By Theorem 2 the whole dependence on the outside is the one scalar γ, so one read of the outside suffices for every pair and every term.

(b) Per term, nothing more is needed than the work of the product inside C plus a fixed number of word operations. *Reason:* Theorem 2: the output mask is (C ∖ D) ∪ Q, one word; the signs are parities of bit counts on words; everything else happens inside C.

(c) When C grows with n, the cost must grow. *Reason:* the output can grow: the output blades of e_1 ⟇ e_2 have grade n or n − 2, so up to 1 + C(n,2) terms, and in the form with 2 on the diagonal and 1 elsewhere e_1 ⟇ e_2 has 2(n − 1) terms for n = 2 to 7, in a second integer family 7, 10, 15 at n = 4, 5, 6, while e_1 ⟑ e_2 keeps 2. A method writes its output, so its cost grows at least as the output.

So, for the use measured in WU7b (C = the four rigid(3) generators at every n): the Θ(n²) loop work per pair is an artifact of conjugating over all n generators, and the 2^(n−1) term-list capacity is an artifact of a bound taken over the complements. The inherent part is one read of the form outside C, which is of the order of the signature data that decision 0005 admits, and which `metric_like` already performs once per algebra. The owner's reading is right in its arithmetic: the grade of a result and its sign need only O(1) word operations on n-bit masks; the Θ(n) per term comes from loops over generators, not from the mathematics.

## 5. The prototype

Because Theorem 2 makes the direct route a change of per-basis data, the owner's prototype step applies. `docs/agent/records/spikes/antiproduct-direct/reduced_antiproduct.hpp` (outside `include/`, built on the headers of `50c7c70`) adds:

- `coupled_closure(g, used)`: the generators coupled to `used`, one row of the form per generator reached;
- `reduction_of(g, used)`: C, Q, and the capacity a component of Q needs;
- `spectator_square<Capacity>(g, Q)`: γ, from `blade_product` of each component of Q with itself;
- `generate_reduced_plan<Capacity>(g, C, γ, as, bs, out)`: WU7b's `generate_plan<antigp>` with complements inside C and the signs of Theorem 2; the same term order and the same coefficient guard;
- `make_reduced_plan` and `reduced_plan<Alg, A, B>`: as `make_product_plan`, every local `static constexpr`, the same defect checks and typing, reusing WU7b's functions;
- `coupling<Alg>` (each generator's component and each component's determinant, once per algebra), `split_from`, and `cached_plan<Alg, A, B>`, the same plan with C and γ taken from `coupling<Alg>` (added for run 2 of section 6).

`equivalence.cpp` checks it. At compile time, on GCC 16.2.1 and Clang 23.1.4 with Galoot's test flags at -O1: `reduced_plan` equals WU7b's `product_plan<antigp>` for the four typed pairs at n = 4, 5, and 8 in R(n−1,0,1), for `full<4>` × `full<4>` in rigid(3) and in conformal(2), and for a conformal(3) use on e1, e2, and the origin, whose closure adds the point at infinity and leaves e3 outside; `cached_plan` likewise for point × point and motor × point at those n and for the conformal(3) use. At run time, for every pair of single blades with both input signs, the reduced generator against `generate_plan<antigp>`, and the per-algebra C and γ against the per-plan ones: every R(p,q,r) with n ≤ 6, conformal(1) to conformal(4), the Witt forms, the degenerate and radical-off-generator forms, and two block forms (94 forms): 0 differing pairs on both compilers (`out/equivalence-g++.txt`, `out/equivalence-clang++.txt`). Three sign mutants of the prototype each make the run-time check fail (45,812, 48,710, and 76,224 differing pairs); a fourth, dropping the sign of a component determinant, fails on 1,876 pairs, all in forms with a two-generator component outside C. The measurement script also checks the equality at n = 4, 8, 12, and 16 for the four pairs before it times anything (`out/growth-50c7c70/agreement.txt`).

## 7. Recommendation

(b): adopt the reduced conjugation as the antiproduct's construction. It is the one mechanism with different per-basis data, so C4 is met by composition and no new machinery needs admitting; the decision that records it (proposed as 0140) must show that composition, which section 1.5 states: the per-basis data are masks and signs per input and output blade and one coefficient per plan, and γ is itself made of `blade_product` squares. It also has to carry the evidence that the reduced plan equals the conjugated one, for which the conjugated plan, the case C = all generators of the same function, remains the reference (C12).

Not (a): the growth is not inherent, so recording it as an exception would admit a defect. Not the diagonal closed form of Theorem 3 as the construction: it is a second pair rule for diagonal forms only. It is worth having as a check, though: a second route to the diagonal antiproduct's sign that shares no code with the conjugation (as `closed_form_right_sign` is for the complements).

What the owner rules on:

1. Whether the growth recorded in decision 0116 is a defect to fix (this note says it is an artifact, so P2 counts it as a defect), and whether to fix it now, in a unit after WU7b's merge, or in WU10 when the public antiproducts are built.
2. Whether decision 0140's showing satisfies C4: conjugation inside the coupling closure, with the coupling graph read from the metric as new per-algebra data.
3. Where the components live: per plan (O(n²) reads per distinct set of used generators) or once per algebra (O(n²) once, then one step per used generator and per outside component; one per-algebra variable template, as `metric_like` is per algebra). Run 2 measured both; per algebra is recommended, because then nothing per plan grows with n, and its one read of the form could join `metric_like`'s pass.

What an implementing unit would do (after WU7b merges; nothing on `feature/core-plan-maps` changes before its review ends):

- In `plan.hpp`: `factor_of` and `run_term` take C and Q (or a small value holding them and γ); `generate_plan<antigp>` computes nothing new per pair beyond the extra signs; `pair_capacity` and `plan_capacity` take C instead of n for the antiproduct; `make_product_plan` computes C, Q, and γ before the count. The antiwedge needs only the check that Q is empty (its plan is otherwise empty).
- Tests: the reduced plan against the conjugation with C = all generators, every pair of every form of the signature set at run time, with forms that put two-generator components outside C (Witt, conformal) and with |Q| odd; the existing golden composition (WU7b D5) unchanged; the sign mutants above as mutation cases.
- A P2 rerun of decision 0116's use, which should show the antiproduct as flat as the product, and the limits rerun, since `full<n>` sets have C = everything and should not change.
- CONVENTIONS does not change: 4.2 still defines the antiproduct, and the reduction is a theorem about it.
